## A budget is a map, not a punishment

A household budget is often introduced as a list of things people must stop buying. That is why many people avoid making one. They imagine a spreadsheet full of blame: the coffee they bought, the taxi fare they forgot, the child who needed shoes, the electricity that ran out before payday.

A useful budget does not begin by scolding a family for being human. It begins by helping the family see what is happening.

Imagine a household in which one person earns a monthly salary, another brings in occasional income from weekend work, and two children need school transport and lunch money. Rent is due near the beginning of the month. Electricity is bought as needed. A relative sometimes needs help. The car needs maintenance, but only when something goes wrong. By the final week, the family is asking the same question: “Where did the money go?”

The money did not disappear in one dramatic moment. It moved through dozens of ordinary decisions and obligations. A budget makes those movements visible before the next month begins.

The goal is not to make every month perfect. The goal is to make fewer decisions in the dark.

## 1. Start with the money that actually reaches you

Begin with take-home income: the amount available after payroll deductions and other compulsory deductions have been taken off. If you budget from gross salary, you may plan to spend money that never reaches your account.

List every dependable source of household income:

- Take-home salary or wages
- Regular pension or benefit income
- Reliable business or self-employment income
- Regular contributions from another household member
- Other dependable income

Do not treat a once-off bonus, uncertain commission, occasional gift or hoped-for side job as guaranteed income. You can record it separately and decide what to do if it arrives. A plan built on uncertain money becomes fragile.

If income changes from month to month, use a cautious planning figure based on a realistic low or typical month, not the best month you have ever had. When more money comes in, the household can assign it deliberately rather than relying on it to cover promises already made.

### Example

Thandi earns R14,800 after deductions. Her partner earns an average of R2,000 from weekend work, but the amount varies. They decide to build their regular budget around R14,800 and treat the weekend income as variable. When it arrives, they first cover any shortfall in essentials, then set aside money for upcoming irregular costs, and only then decide whether anything can be used for an extra purchase.

This is not pessimism. It is a way of keeping the household plan steady when income is uneven.

## 2. Find out where the money has been going

Before cutting expenses, observe them. For one month, keep a simple record of what leaves the household.

Use a notebook, a spreadsheet, a phone note or bank statements. The tool matters less than the habit. Record cash purchases too; cash can be especially difficult to remember after several small transactions.

For each expense, write:

- Date
- Amount
- What it was for
- Whether it was essential, flexible or optional
- Whether it happens regularly or only sometimes

Do not make the first week a performance. Record honestly. If you hide an expense from the budget, you are hiding it from the people who need the information—including your future self.

At the end of the month, group expenses into categories. Common household categories include housing, electricity and water, groceries, transport, school and childcare, communication, medical costs, insurance, debt payments, savings, family support, personal spending and occasional costs.

South African consumer education and financial-literacy material commonly recommends listing income and expenses, including fixed and variable costs, and comparing planned spending with actual spending. The point is not to use someone else’s categories exactly. It is to create a picture that fits your household.

## 3. Separate fixed, variable and occasional costs

A budget becomes easier to manage when expenses are grouped by how they behave.

### Fixed or committed costs

These are payments that are relatively predictable or contractually committed: rent, a bond payment, school fees, insurance premiums, instalment payments, subscriptions and certain transport costs.

Some fixed costs can change over time, but you usually know the amount or due date in advance.

### Variable essentials

These are necessary expenses whose amounts change: groceries, electricity, water, fuel, public transport, toiletries, household supplies and some medical costs.

These categories need realistic estimates. Underestimating groceries by R1,000 does not create a saving; it creates a budget that fails.

### Occasional and seasonal costs

These costs may not arrive every month, but they are not necessarily surprises: school uniforms, annual licence renewals, holiday travel, vehicle services, home repairs, birthdays, winter clothing or annual insurance payments.

A cost that happens once a year is still part of the household’s yearly cost of living. If you know it is coming, give it a place in the plan.

## 4. Convert irregular costs into monthly amounts

Suppose a household expects the following costs over a year:

| Expense | Expected annual amount |
|---|---:|
| Vehicle service and maintenance | R4,800 |
| School uniforms and supplies | R2,400 |
| Annual licences and renewals | R1,200 |
| Family celebrations | R1,800 |
| **Total** | **R10,200** |

Divide the total by 12: R10,200 ÷ 12 = R850 per month.

The household can set aside R850 each month in a separate savings pocket or account if available. When the annual cost arrives, the money has already been planned for.

This is sometimes called a sinking fund. It is not an emergency fund: it is money for costs you expect, even if you do not know the exact date.

Start with the expenses most likely to disrupt the household. You do not need to fund every category perfectly in the first month. Begin with one or two important upcoming costs and build the habit.

## 5. Build the first monthly budget

Now create a plan using take-home income and the expenses you have recorded.

A useful order is:

1. Housing and basic utilities
2. Food and household essentials
3. Transport needed for work, school and essential responsibilities
4. Medical and care needs
5. Required debt and contractual payments
6. Provision for known upcoming costs
7. A realistic amount for savings or a buffer, if possible
8. Flexible personal and recreation spending

This is a planning order, not a universal rule for every crisis or contract. If you cannot cover essentials and required payments, do not pretend that a normal budget can solve the problem by itself. Contact relevant providers early, seek reputable advice, and check what assistance is available.

### Worked example: a household earning R16,800

The figures below are fictional and illustrative. They are not recommended amounts for every family.

| Monthly item | Planned amount |
|---|---:|
| Take-home income | R16,800 |
| Rent | R5,200 |
| Electricity and water | R1,000 |
| Groceries and household supplies | R3,400 |
| Transport | R2,100 |
| School and childcare | R1,100 |
| Phone and data | R550 |
| Medical and personal care | R700 |
| Insurance | R650 |
| Required debt payments | R1,200 |
| Known irregular costs fund | R500 |
| Emergency buffer/savings | R300 |
| Family and personal spending | R600 |
| **Total planned expenses** | **R17,300** |
| **Monthly gap** | **–R500** |

The household has discovered a R500 gap before the month begins. That is valuable information. It is better to see the problem on paper than to discover it after rent and food have already been paid.

The next step is not to erase the gap by writing “spend less” beside the total. The household must decide which changes are realistic and which commitments need attention.

They might review the grocery plan, compare transport options, check whether a phone/data package still suits their use, pause a genuinely optional expense, or look for a way to increase dependable income. They should not remove necessary medicine, food, safe housing or essential transport simply to make the worksheet balance.

If the household can reduce flexible costs by R200 and the variable weekend income reliably contributes another R300 in a particular month, they may cover the gap that month. But they should not build recurring obligations around income that is uncertain.

## 6. Give every rand a job—without pretending every rand is available

A budget works best when money is assigned before it is spent. That does not mean every rand must be locked away in a complicated system. It means the household decides what each portion is meant to cover.

Some households use separate bank accounts or savings pockets. Others use envelopes, a written list or a spreadsheet. Choose a system you can maintain.

On payday, identify:

- Payments due before the next payday
- Food and transport money for the period
- Money needed for school, care and essential household items
- Contributions to known upcoming costs
- Any amount available for a buffer or savings
- A modest amount each person can use without having to renegotiate every small purchase

If two adults share expenses, agree on the plan together. One person should not be expected to manage the household budget while the other is kept unaware of the numbers. The budget is a shared picture of shared responsibilities.

## 7. Make the budget fit the pay cycle

A monthly budget can still fail if the household receives money weekly, fortnightly or on different dates.

Write down when income arrives and when each bill is due. Then create a calendar.

For example, if rent is due on the first and salary arrives on the twenty-fifth, the rent money needs to be protected immediately. If transport and groceries are needed every week, divide the monthly plan into weekly amounts.

A simple conversion is:

- Monthly amount × 12 ÷ 52 = approximate weekly amount
- Weekly amount × 52 ÷ 12 = approximate monthly amount

This is more accurate than simply dividing by four, because a year contains more than 48 weeks. For everyday planning, rounding is acceptable; for bills and contracts, use the actual due dates and amounts.

## 8. What to do when the budget does not balance

If expenses are greater than income, the budget is not a failure. It has revealed a real problem.

Work through the gap carefully:

**First, check the numbers.** Did you forget a deduction, a regular contribution, a debt payment or a school cost? Are annual costs included? Are you using take-home income?

**Second, protect essentials.** Identify housing, food, necessary utilities, health needs, work and school transport, and care responsibilities. Do not make cuts that create danger or undermine essential needs.

**Third, review flexible spending.** Look for expenses that can be reduced, replaced, delayed or cancelled without causing a larger cost later. Small changes can help, but do not expect a few minor purchases to solve a large structural shortfall.

**Fourth, address commitments early.** If you cannot make a payment, contact the provider before the due date where possible. Ask what options exist and get any agreement in writing. Do not ignore letters or assume that silence pauses a contract.

**Fifth, seek qualified support when needed.** In South Africa, the National Credit Regulator provides consumer information and guidance about credit and debt counselling. If debt repayments are unmanageable, contact the NCR or a properly registered debt counsellor to understand your options. Debt counselling has legal and practical consequences, so get clear information before entering an arrangement.

A budget cannot create income that does not exist. It can help you understand the shortfall, make better decisions, and explain your situation when seeking assistance.

## 9. Hold a short household money meeting

Once a week, take 15 minutes to review the plan. Keep the meeting calm and practical.

Ask:

- What bills or expenses are coming before the next meeting?
- How much remains for groceries and transport?
- Did an unexpected cost appear?
- Is any category running ahead of plan?
- What decision do we need to make together?

Do not turn the meeting into a trial about who spent what. Focus on what the household needs to do next.

If a child needs shoes, a family member becomes ill, or a work shift changes, the budget should be adjusted. A budget is a living plan, not a promise that life will remain unchanged.

## 10. Your 30-day household budget reset

### Days 1–7: Observe
Record every expense. Gather payslips, bank statements, bills, debit orders and debt payment information. Do not rush to judge.

### Days 8–14: Organise
Group spending into categories. Separate fixed, variable, occasional and optional costs. List annual and seasonal expenses.

### Days 15–21: Plan
Use take-home income. Set realistic amounts for essentials, commitments, known upcoming costs and flexible spending. Calculate the difference between income and planned expenses.

### Days 22–30: Practise and adjust
Follow the plan. Review spending once a week. If a category is unrealistic, revise it rather than abandoning the whole budget. At the end of the month, compare planned and actual amounts and make the next month more accurate.

## Closing: clarity is a form of control

A household budget will not make every salary larger or every bill smaller. It will not prevent illness, unemployment, price increases or every family emergency. But it can replace uncertainty with information.

When the numbers are visible, a family can decide together. It can prepare for known costs, recognise a shortfall earlier, protect essential needs and ask for help with a clearer understanding of the problem.

The first budget does not have to be beautiful. It has to be honest. The second budget can be more accurate. The third can be more useful still.

Start with the money you have, the responsibilities you carry, and the month in front of you. That is enough to begin.
